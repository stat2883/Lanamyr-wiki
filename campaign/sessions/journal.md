# Campaign Journal

A running, player-facing recap. One short entry per session, newest at the
bottom. Written to be read — no stat blocks, no mechanics, no DM notes.

For the detailed record of any session, see the numbered files in this
directory. For current party state, see `../campaign-status.md`.

## Session 0 — Setup

*No play. Caldalus, 387 AW, still ahead of us.*

We settled on the Elf War. Not the middle of it, not the aftermath — the
beginning, 387 AW, when Faelyn's militia was still small enough that the
sergeants knew every name and none of them had ever fought a war.

Two brothers came out of it. **Sharii** and **Alinar**, Wood Elf twins, both
forty-five, both unremarkable enough that you couldn't pick either one out of a
crowd. Alinar is the younger by minutes and the one who decides things. Sharii
is the elder and has spent his life following him.

They grew up in Caldalus. When the Stone Giants started coming down out of the
north, they watched the messengers arrive, and then the refugees, asking the
city for help. Alinar wanted to lend a hand. Sharii went with him, because
that's what Sharii does.

Alinar came out of it the sharper weapon — two blades, quick, and quiet enough
to move through the trees without anyone hearing. Sharii came out of it
something stranger: the better eyes, the steadier voice, the only healing hands
between them, and a longbow he is only passably good with. He also came out of
it wearing scale mail, which Neva Corrin will not let him forget.

And there is the other thing about Sharii, the one neither brother has a word
for. Magic has always leaned toward him. He never wanted it. He never learned
it. He chose a bow and a brother instead, and it has never entirely left him
alone.

They'll be assigned to a squadron of about twenty. Among them: a sergeant named
Serwyn Thale who is a fine drillmaster and has never seen a battle, a farm boy
from the south named Ohren Kade, a gnome scout named Wick Pallowen, a young
elf named Tam Ellisan who enlisted because his friends did — and two other new
recruits from the same intake. A quiet man called Kharis Ailwin, who walked to
Caldalus from a village that isn't there anymore.

And a woman with green eyes, which is uncommon among Wood Elves, who would very
much prefer that nobody mention whose daughter she is.

*Next: the recruitment line.*

## Session 1 — Ashwell

*387 AW. Caldalus to the Tempest foothills to a burning village and back.*

They stood in the recruitment line and told Sergeant Thale the truth, more or
less: foragers, hunters, the occasional bear when one got too curious. Thale
didn't laugh. He wrote it down and put them where self-sufficiency mattered
more than swordwork.

The first night in the barracks, Sharii found the woman with green eyes and
learned nothing of her family and something of her reasons — she's here to
become something she chose instead of something she was handed, same as him.
She warned him, gently, not to go digging at the quiet man's history. Alinar
found the gnome, Wick, and discovered a partner in the specific, particular
art of not being seen. And somewhere in the middle of an evening full of
stew and bad jokes, Sharii wore his scale mail like an argument and won it —
Neva Corrin, the squadron's best archer, decided he was **bear bait**, said
it like an insult, and hasn't managed to make it sound like one since.

Weeks of drill went by in a blur of forms and marches, the way training does.
Then, word came down: Stone Giants, closer than anyone alive remembers them
coming. Not a drill. A patrol, into ground that might have something looking
back.

They found the first track on the second day — a footprint the size of a
cart wheel, a day old, heading the wrong direction: toward people, not away
from them. Alinar found a second track nobody else had caught, smaller,
staggered half a day behind the first. Two giants. Not walking together.
Nobody knew why, and there wasn't time to wonder — a small farming
settlement called Ashwell sat directly in their path, closer than home.

They pushed the last daylight hard and got there anyway too late to stop the
burning. Smoke over a barn and a grain store. Two giants already inside the
fences, and villagers running for a treeline that wasn't nearly far enough.

What followed was the worst two minutes any of them had lived through. Sharii
ran straight at one of them, loud on purpose, bear bait for real this time,
and led it away from the fleeing villagers into an ambush that very nearly
wasn't fast enough — he went down under a blow that should have killed him,
came back from the edge of it on nothing but luck, and was on his feet again
before the giant even finished falling. Alinar closed to blades and helped
finish what the arrows started. Somewhere behind them, Mya Li stood alone
over an unconscious Neva Corrin and held a second giant off her with nothing
but her own two blades and whatever it is that makes her the kind of person
who does that, and didn't move until it was dead too.

By the time the smoke cleared, both giants were down. Nobody in the squadron
died. Sharii healed Neva back to her feet, and the first thing out of her
mouth was an insult, which everyone took as a good sign.

Ashwell was not so lucky, and they only found out properly the next morning.
Four of its people had died before the squadron ever came over the rise — a
woman caught in the open going for the trees, an old man who would not leave
his house, and two brothers who went into the grain store after the seed corn
and did not come out of it. The store burned anyway. Forty-five people lived
in Ashwell on the morning the giants came through, and forty-one lived in it
afterward.

The village headman, a farmer named Aldric, gave them floor space for the
night and more gratitude than either brother knew what to do with, and he had
not lost anybody of his own, and that is the part he could not seem to put
down.

Somewhere in the trees near the ruined fences, a black wolf pup watched the
whole thing from a safe distance and, for reasons of his own, decided not to
leave. Sharii named him Knight.

*Next: whatever Ashwell needs in the morning — and whatever's out there that
made two giants leave home at the same time, going the same way, alone.*

## Session 2 — The Hall Under the Tempest Peaks

*387 AW. Ashwell, a hole in a mountain, and a captain's office in Caldalus.*

Morning after the giants. Alinar went round Ashwell with a tally and came back
with two problems: the village had lost its seed corn along with its bread, and
two of its people weren't in it. An old woman named Hestra Fenn and her grandson
Coll, nine years old, gone up the north draw with the goats the day before the
giants came through.

Sharii spent a spell talking to the wolf, which turned out to be the single most
useful thing anyone did all day. Knight's mother had moved the den four or five
nights before any giant came near — moved them downhill, at night, one at a
time, frightened in a way her pup had never smelled on her before. Not of
giants. He has a whole separate smell for giants. Of something else that had
come through the ridge on two legs and never made a sound. Cold, he said. It
smelled of being *underneath*.

Sharii told that to Thale in front of everyone and added that an animal hasn't
got the equipment to lie, and that everyone else standing in the yard had.
Thale took the wolf as a witness. Then Sharii turned to Mya and asked her
opinion first, by name, before anyone else's, which she understood perfectly
and said nothing about.

They went up the draw. They found the goats, and a dead one bled clean with a
single haunch taken, and the boy wedged in a gap between two boulders where he
had been for thirty hours because his grandmother told him to stay there. And
they found a crack in the ridge that wasn't a crack — chisel work decades old,
steps cut into the floor, and warm air coming out of it in autumn.

Six of them went down. The stair came out onto a corridor that was already
there: dressed stone, three hundred and fifty feet of it, writing cut at chest
height the whole length in a hand none of them could read. At the end of it a
collapse, with a crawl pulled through and the stones stacked tidily to one side,
and on the far side of twelve feet of rubble somebody tapping a rock on the
floor. Three strikes. Pause. Over and over, for a day and a half, by a blind
old woman with a broken ankle who had decided that was what you did.

Alinar went in first, on his stomach. Sharii followed, because that's what
Sharii does. Mya took off her chain shirt and came third. Knight would not go
near the hole and nothing on earth was going to make him.

What was on the other side was not a cave. Pillars going off further than
anyone's eyes reached, a floor and walls carved over with the same writing,
and the air warm and dry and dead still. Hestra was propped against a post with
water set by one hand and food by the other and three fresh scratches cut into
the stone above her head. They'd taken her, waited when her ankle went, never
said one word to her or to each other, left her alive, and gone off into the
dark. *You don't water a thing you've finished with*, she said. *They're coming
back for me.*

They got her out. On the way, something a hundred feet off in the pillars
switched a light on — pale, steady, no flame, sitting over a stone box somebody
had carried down there and set on the floor inside a circle of freshly cut
writing. Then a second light, further out. Then a third. The second one went
dark and came on again closer without ever crossing the ground in between.
Alinar was the last man into the crawl and got the only clear look at any of it.
Behind them, as they formed up to run, somebody set a stone down neatly on stone
at the far end of the hole.

Nothing followed them. Three hundred and fifty feet of corridor, forty of stair,
and out into grey daylight and Neva Corrin's drawn bow. They walked through
Ashwell and kept walking, in the dark, four days to Caldalus.

The report did not go well at first. Captain Vaeroth has four hundred years of
survey telling him there is nothing under the Tempest Peaks but rock, and three
recruits, a sergeant and a blind woman telling him otherwise. Mya argued it on
the arithmetic — if we're wrong you waste a scouting party, if we're right and
you do nothing then in six months something comes out of a door in our own
border that we knew about. He asked her whether that was her assessment or her
father's. She said she hadn't spoken to her father in six weeks.

Then Sharii called her the princess, in front of the captain, and the room went
cold — and instead of backing out of it he went further in. He told Vaeroth
about the thing that happens to him. The presence. Less than a dozen times in a
life, never pleasant, always leaving him weak, and twice in one day under that
mountain. And then he said, to her and not to the captain, that he hadn't
invoked her title for its weight; he'd done it because she had put her name at
risk by opening her mouth at all, and somebody ought to say so.

It was a reckless thing to do and it worked. Vaeroth didn't laugh. He said his
grandmother had had something like it, and that he wasn't going to pretend it
wasn't real in front of three witnesses who'd watched it work, and that he
wasn't going to write it down either — because the first tactician who reads
*the scout had a feeling* stops reading. He told Sharii plainly that covering
him and using him were the same act.

What actually turned the captain was Alinar, reciting a flat list with no
adjectives in it. Raiders take the whole goat; these ones bled it and took a
haunch, which is rationing, which means they mean to stay. They don't speak,
which means either they can't or they've a way of speaking you can't hear. They
left a prisoner watered against a marked post, which is not mercy and not
carelessness — that's a thing set aside to be collected. And they're installing
equipment, in a line, inside a building they can read and you can't.

*They're not squatters, Sergeant. They're a working party. Somebody sent them,
somebody is supplying them, and somebody expects a result.*

And the hall runs north. Under the Peaks. The direction everything has come
from since midsummer.

Survey party authorised. Twenty, with engineers, inside the week. A second
letter over the captain's own seal to Faelyn's tacticians saying that in his
judgement the giants are not the whole of what is happening to us. And Ashwell's
grain submitted as a requisition rather than a request.

Eight days in the capital after that. Sharii spent his mornings with the wolf
and the rest of his slots on it, and came out the other end with a set of hand
signals Knight will answer without any magic at all — and the discovery that the
animal can tell when the thing happens to him. His afternoons went on getting
out of the scale mail, which took a leatherworker Bellis knows, all of Alinar's
money, eight gold from Neva Corrin that she will deny to her grave, and a hazard
payment Thale appears to have invented on the spot.

Alinar spent his eight days on paper. It turns out Ashwell isn't a chartered
settlement — four families on crown ground — and crown grain comes out against
charters, and there isn't a box on the form for forty-one people who simply
exist. He went back to the same desk four days running and read the same list of
names until somebody dealt with him. He got forty days of flour out of a militia
column, and then, by accident on the fifth day, he found a different store under
a different office with a box on the form for crown ground under cultivation.

Seed corn. Ashwell can plant in the spring. That was the one that was going to
kill them, and a clerk named Ivell Sarrow told him the rest of the answer on the
way out: get them attached to a bigger village's roll and they stop being
invisible. A place called Dorn's Ford, a day east, with a mill.

*Next: back up the draw. Twenty surveyors and engineers, the whole squadron as
escort, the flour and the seed corn on the carts — and Captain Vaeroth coming
up that mountain himself.*

## Interlude — The Granary Yard

*387 AW. Caldalus, the night before the column marches north.*

The reinforcements came up from the southern holdings in the rain, three days
on the road, and nobody in Caldalus was expecting them this week. The clerk at
the gate wrote the names onto the wrong roll. The first barracks was full. So
was the second.

Which is how a Tiefling named **Ignis** ended up in the mud behind the granary
at an hour when a city ought to be asleep, in a yard full of lantern light and
soldiers loading sacks onto carts for a dawn march, being told to ask for a man
called Ordow.

She went to the wolf first. Before either of the two men, before the
quartermaster with the tally board — she put her hand out to a four-month-old
black wolf in the rain and called him handsome.

Knight did not come to her hand. He got up, crossed four feet of mud, took her
in properly, and sat down just out of reach, and that is not a thing anybody in
this squadron has seen him do. He either makes a fool of himself or he gets
behind Sharii. He does not sit down and consider.

*"That's new,"* Sharii said.

Ordow took her name for the roll and asked the only two questions he ever asks:
can you walk four days, and what are you *for*. She told him she was here as
support for the escort squadron. He wrote it down exactly as she said it, read
it back to himself, and told her without any unkindness at all that support was
an honest word, and also a word that lets a person not say a thing. Then he
left a space beside it on the board and went back to counting sacks.

Alinar came through the gate at a fast walk with a document case pressed dry
under his coat, wanting to know whether the seed corn was on the second cart
and under the sheet. He looked at her for the same half-second his brother had,
recovered the same way, and did not ask the question Ordow had just left
hanging. He told her about Ashwell instead — forty-one people, a lost barn,
forty days of flour, and twenty-two armed strangers about to land on them —
and said that most of the squadron thought the mountain was the hard part.

Then, on his way to the carts, he told her how to read Ordow. *He writes down
what people tell him and then he watches to see if it was true. He's been doing
it two hundred years and he's very rarely surprised.* A pause. *He likes being
surprised.*

When she turned for the tack room, the wolf got up and walked her there. Four
feet off her left side, all the way across the yard, with Sharii watching from
his crate and not calling him back. At the step down Knight stopped, looked
into the dark, looked at her — and then trotted back and went to sleep across
his master's boots.

Dawn came grey and still raining. Twenty surveyors and engineers. Three carts
under sheets. A sergeant with a list, a captain who had never once come along
before, and a column forming up in the mud, pointed north at a mountain.

*Next: four days to Ashwell.*

## Session 3 — The Ledge

*387 AW. Nine days. Caldalus to a sealed door, and what was behind it.*

It started at the gate in the rain, with the captain on a horse counting
nineteen and finding twenty. He asked the Tiefling what she was for and got an
answer — because Sharii volunteered one on her behalf and was wrong about it,
and she corrected him flat in front of the column. *I'm a druid. And I'm great
for healing, which you're going to need.*

Vaeroth put her mid-column, ordered the whole squadron to learn her face by
nightfall, and told her to her face that she had just become the most valuable
body in it and the one everything would run toward when it went wrong.

Four days north. Alinar walked beside her and handed over everything he knew
about Ashwell without being asked for any of it. Sharii spent a spell asking a
wolf what he made of her and got back that she smells like the far side of a
fire, that she *was still arriving*, and that she is one of the ones — of which
there appear to be about six. Every night she dried the ground under forty
bedrolls and laid fog around the camp, until the engineers started putting
themselves near her without discussing it.

Ashwell stood in the road and counted them coming, which is what a village of
forty-one does when twenty-two armed strangers appear on the horizon. Then
Aldric found Alinar's face in the column and the relief that went through him
was not a thing anyone wanted to watch. He took the flour without reacting. He
had already done the arithmetic on forty days and knew it was January. Then
Alinar said *seed corn*, and the man put his hand over his eyes and left it
there. *We'd have eaten it. Come March, we'd have eaten the seed and known we
were doing it.*

Nobody slept in the village. They camped outside the walls and refused
everything offered, and the village kept putting things down anyway. Alinar and
Wick split the flour four ways instead of stacking it in one building, because
last time it was all in one barn and a giant stepped on the barn. Ignis spent
the evening setting an old woman's ankle properly and then teaching a
nine-year-old to do it himself for the next three weeks — and his grandmother
took her aside afterward and said *be careful what you tell him, lass. He does
what he's told. All the way down.*

Then up the draw. And the door was shut.

Not fallen in. **Shut** — cut stone, fitted dry, keyed into the rock, two feet
thick, laid from the inside by somebody who took the rubble away with them. The
goats were gone. And beside it, cut fresh into the mountain, were three
scratches: the same mark they'd found over Hestra Fenn's head in the dark.

What they had was an engineer, a wolf, a gnome who notices draughts, and a
druid who can make ten gallons of water out of nothing. What they did with it
took two nights and a day. Oak wedges in the joints, kept wet so they swelled.
Every crack in the face flooded at dusk so the frost could split it overnight —
and the stone ticked in the dark like something cooling. Then a bonfire jammed
against it all day, which Ignis turned violet and green for an hour for no
reason except that forty people had been carrying wood since dawn. Then the
quench, and a noise like a dropped anvil, and a face crazed white like the
glaze on an old cup.

In between, they went over the shoulder onto the north face, where no Wood Elf
has walked in living memory. They found the far end of it: a patch of green
grass with no frost on it and a four-inch crack breathing warm air. They found
the goats' tracks — driven out alive, bunched, with long narrow footprints
walking the outside of the herd like a drover. And they found one of *them*,
four hundred yards off, walking downhill on a route it clearly knew, wrapped
and veiled with not an inch of skin showing.

They had it. Six of them, invisible, with the best archer in the company lying
flat and ready. They talked about taking it, and worked out what it would
cost, and let it walk — and Mya made the argument that decided it, which was
that these things don't speak, so there would have been no interrogation, only
a corpse and a kingdom that had lost its quiet. Then they stayed put four more
minutes and got paid for it: a second one came up the same path, and the two
passed each other at six feet without a nod, a glance, or any acknowledgement
at all.

And somewhere in those two days Alinar reached for everything he knew about
Dark Elves and found a hole where the knowledge should be. No face. No colour.
Nothing but a children's story about a place under the world. And at the bottom
of that hole, the thing he has never once in forty-five years thought to ask:
*he speaks their language.* Both brothers do. Somebody taught them, as
children, deliberately, for years.

The door came open on the seventh day.

What was behind it was not a corridor. Past the junction, down the left-hand
passage nobody had ever walked, there was a line of river stones laid out every
thirty feet like a measure — in a mountain with no river. There was a depot:
water, rope, tools laid out by size, and forty or fifty folded veils stacked
like linen in a cupboard. There was the place where the old writing **stopped
mid-line**, and eight feet on where the dressed stone stopped too, and beyond
that raw rock, freshly cut, still being cut — with the inscription already
being carved into the new walls, picking up exactly where the old sentence
ended.

*They're not digging it out,* the engineer said. *They're finishing it.*

And then the tunnel opened, and there was no far wall and no ceiling and the
floor was a very long way down, and on it were fifty pale lights standing
absolutely still in long arcs, like the lamps of a city seen from a hill —
except that cities don't lay their lamps in arcs and their lights flicker. One
whole arc of them was still dark.

Eighty feet along the ledge, nine of them were cutting a staircase down into
it. In total darkness. In total silence. Two of them working a face fifteen
inches apart, passing tools back and forth, and **never once looking at each
other.**

They were six foot four and thin as wire, with pale hair tied back and long
hands and no weapons of any kind. And they had already put fifty lamps down
there — which means they got down some other way, which means the staircase
isn't for them.

So the party turned round and left, and that would have been the end of it,
except the engineer slipped.

All nine stopped at once. Three seconds, each where they stood, facing nothing.
Then they went back to work at exactly the rate they'd been working — and the
one furthest away, facing entirely the wrong direction, put down its basket and
started walking.

It followed them five hundred feet up a corridor at a walking pace. Never
closed. Never fell behind. Never called out, because it has nothing to call
with, and never drew anything, because it was carrying nothing at all.

They held the junction to let Maevis take her copy of the wall. At thirty feet
Alinar challenged it and it stopped. And in the lantern light they finally saw
one properly: white skin you could see the veins through, white hair, and
enormous black eyes with no whites in them at all.

It crouched, with both empty hands in plain view, and put a stone on the floor
halfway between them. Then it stood, and stepped back three paces, and waited.

Ignis walked up and took it. Close to, it breathes about four times a minute
and its hands are a working man's — split nails, old scars, callus like
saddle leather. The stone was warm from somebody's pocket and worn smooth from
being carried. She bowed. It didn't bow back; you don't bow to someone who
isn't looking at you.

Then it turned and walked away — and eighty feet out, at the edge of the light,
**it stopped and turned its head**, which is the first time anyone has ever seen
one of them do that at all.

Nobody can agree what it was looking at. Neva swears it was Sharii. Wick says
the wolf. Mya says the stair. Sharii saw it and hasn't said.

They came up into daylight with no injuries, a wax rubbing of a dead language,
and a warm river stone made of rock that doesn't exist on this mountain.

The captain heard it twice through and then said the thing nobody wanted
spelled out: *that door was shut, and we opened it, and we cannot shut it
again.* He is writing to the king. He has never written to a king.

And they decided to answer.

Not with gold — that only says what you can be bought with. Not with food —
that's either tribute or what you put out for a dog, and you can't tell which.
And above all not with a stone, because you cannot read their writing and you
do not sign a document in a language you don't speak.

Sharii thought of the wax, and gave the decision to Mya, who took it.

*The chisel's the right answer,* she said. *And a thing that cannot go wrong
cannot do anything either. We don't have the room for that.*

So at first light on the ninth day, six of them went back down and left a blank
wax tablet on the floor of the junction, with the stylus seated in its spine
and one short stroke cut in the corner — the mark a mason makes to check the
tool is taking, which says *this is a surface and this is how you mark it* and
says nothing else in any language.

Then the whole column walked off that mountain and left it lying there.

Knight was the only one who looked back.

*Next: four days home, and a week in which nobody is allowed up that draw.*

## Session 4 — Dorn's Ford

*387 AW. Nine days out to seventeen. A spell on a frozen slope, an old blind
woman's testimony, and a miller.*

They were ten minutes down the draw when Ignis went up the column against the
flow of it and asked the captain whether he wanted her to spend a slot finding
out whether the tablet was still lying where they'd left it.

Vaeroth stopped walking. He asked one question — *does it tell them you're
doing it* — and on being told no, halted forty-two people on a frozen slope and
stood them in the cold for ten minutes so one person could shut her eyes. *Don't
give me the good answer. Give me the one you get.*

Maevis did the counting. Behind, and below, and not moving. One minute. Three.
Five. Seven, and by seven everybody standing there had quietly decided they knew
the answer and didn't much like it.

**At eight minutes it moved.** Away. Down, and north — not up the stair — at a
walking pace, in no hurry at all. And at somewhere past nine and a half it was
simply gone, because forty-two people had walked her out of range of it while it
was still going.

Nobody came for it for the better part of half an hour, and then somebody did.
*That's not a patrol finding it*, Maevis said. *Somebody went and fetched it.*

And Mya, who hadn't said anything: *that's not them tolerating us. That's them
answering the post.*

Ignis told the captain afterward, without being asked, that she could never have
seen *who*. Only that it started moving. To see who, somebody has to be sitting
in that junction in the dark with their eyes open, and he said plainly that he
would not order that this month, this winter, or on his own authority at all.

---

In Ashwell, Alinar told Aldric everything flat and in order — the door that
won't close, the people who have never raised a hand, and the goats, alive and
penned and *fed*, which is the word the man couldn't get past. *You don't feed a
stolen goat. You eat it.*

Then Aldric asked whether *no danger* was the captain's word or Alinar's, and
Alinar wouldn't answer it. He sent him to Hestra instead — the only one who'd
actually been in their hands.

They sat on that bench a long time. What came out of it was the thing a blind
woman knows and nobody else could: there were three of them, she counted their
breathing, and one was lighter on the floor than the others. And the first time
they set water down for her it was a hand's width past her reach, and she went
hunting along the stone for it and nearly had it over — and every time after
that, it was exactly where her hand came down.

They watched her fail once, and then they never let her fail again.

*I've been fed by people in my life and I've been kept by people, and those are
different, and I know which one that was.*

Aldric came back up the lane and gave three rulings. Nobody goes up the draw.
The north meadow gets grazed in the spring anyway. And he tells the whole
village, all of it, because sitting on a thing is how a headman turns into a
liar.

Wick and Dalla got the hill. The party wrote their orders themselves and the
captain added three lines to them. Ignis found a nine-year-old had been
re-splinting his grandmother's ankle every day for a fortnight at exactly the
same hour, to exactly the mark he'd been shown, on a leg that had got thinner —
and had rubbed it raw, and had checked his work twice as taught, and had carried
on, because nobody told him to stop.

And the night before they split, she turned into a wolf and fought Knight in the
frozen grass for an hour until Ohren Kade had to sit down laughing. At the end
of it the pup went and fetched a deer bone and put it on the ground exactly
halfway between her and Sharii, and stepped back, and sat.

*It's a puppy,* Wick said loudly, *with a bone. That's what they do.*

*It is,* Mya agreed, in a voice that agreed with nothing.

Nobody picked it up.

---

The twins didn't go east. Alinar laid out everything he'd spent a fortnight
collecting, handed it to four other people, and walked west with the column. He
told Aldric it wasn't his forte, and Aldric told him to his face that was a lie,
and a kind one, so he'd allow it.

Dorn's Ford has a mill, two hundred people, and a river. Aldric stopped in the
road a hundred yards out and said *they'll know me*, and they did.

He asked badly and then honestly, with his hat in his hands, in the open, in
front of a dozen people — including the part about his grandfather walking out
and there being no way to say any of it that didn't mean *we couldn't manage*.

Merrick Dorn didn't gloat. He said it was the first honest thing said in that
yard since the spring, and that he'd have signed it twenty years ago at that
table in an afternoon, and that it wasn't twenty years ago. Then he laid out why
he couldn't: a roll is a list of who *owes*. Ashwell on his book meant his barn
feeding them through a winter he hadn't the grain for, and two more of his boys
going north to a war, and he could name them, and one has a wife expecting in
March.

*And I'd still rather find a way than tell you no in front of my own people. So
somebody had better give me one, because I can't.*

So Ignis asked him whether anybody here had noticed the wildlife coming down off
the mountain.

A woman by the weighbeam said *Ha*, once, like a cough. Best autumn for game in
living memory. Snare lines full every morning since the leaves turned. Roe in
the stubble in daylight that barely move for you. A boar in the orchard twice.
And Jorrun went three days up into the hills a fortnight back because that's
where you go, and came back with two birds and a face on him.

*Everything's here. Nothing's there.*

And Merrick Dorn stood very still and said, to nobody, *the wheat* — because
he's a miller, and he'd just worked out that he had deer in his winter corn all
through the hungry months and had spent a season thanking the gods for it.

Then he asked what they knew and where from, and warned them he'd take it for a
threat if it came out wrong.

She gave him all of it. The hall, the silent people, the door the militia opened
and cannot shut, the goats, the letter on its way to a king, the pulse under the
mountain that has emptied it of everything that walks. That a fox told her, and
a crow, and that she'd rather say it plainly than be caught dressing it up. Then
the trade: you have too much meat and no bread to spare, they have no meat and
forty days of flour, let them take game under your hedges and your wheat gets
guarded all winter by the hungriest people in the valley and you never open your
barn.

And then the thing that actually turned him, which wasn't an argument at all —
that she'd spent an evening teaching a nine-year-old to set a bone, because she
wasn't going to be there. *I'd rather be the one who taught the boy than the one
who was needed.*

*I believe you,* he said. *And I want that noted by everyone stood here. Because
nobody inventing a tale tells you the part that's their own fault.*

The game he gave them outright. The levy he could not, until Thale — who had
promised to stand in that yard in uniform and say nothing, and who asked her
permission first, and who announced out loud that he was breaking his word
before he broke it — produced a clause older than the war: a village that houses
and feeds a crown post is relieved against its levy by the heads it supports.
There are two militia going on that hill tomorrow. *That's a post.* He can't
grant it. He can put his name on the recommendation, over his own captain's
signature, and he said so without selling it as anything more.

And Dovin opened his case and pointed out the thing nobody had thought of: no
clerk amends a roll against a village with no drawn boundary, and the north edge
of a crown plan carries the neighbour's name on it, in ink, forever.

The ink was out before dark. The game, outright. The boundary, surveyed. And the
charter — signed, and conditional, and it lapses in March if the crown says no.

*That's not me hedging against you, Aldric. That's me being able to stand in the
chapel on Sunday.*

Aldric stopped once in the lane on the way back and stood there a moment, and
then carried on.

---

The column reached Caldalus on the thirteenth day and the others came in four
days behind it, in the first snow. The captain's letter to the king went while
they were still walking — he wrote it four times and sent the shortest — and it
has not one word in it about a levy or a charter or a miller, because none of it
had happened yet.

Maevis lost the rubbing. A scholar couldn't read it, couldn't place it, didn't
believe it was descended from anything he knew, and asked to keep it; she said
no; and three days later somebody much more senior asked rather less politely,
and it went up the chain and out of her hands.

The old letter to the tacticians finally got its answer: four lines, thanking
the captain for his diligence, and noting that the kingdom's attention is
presently on the eastern approaches.

Which is where the war was, that week. A mixed probe out of the east, two
villages touched, a company engaged, dead on both sides. Nobody they knew. It
was the first time in a fortnight anyone in the barracks had talked about the
actual war, and it landed oddly, like remembering an appointment.

Alinar met them at the gate, because he'd been going to the gate for two days,
and asked only whether he'd signed. Then he worked through the whole shape of it
out loud — a village on a book because of a petition nobody's written, over the
signature of a captain who hasn't heard of it, in a war that just took two
villages — and concluded that it was a better position than they'd had.

Thale didn't stop in the yard at all. He went straight past everyone, with the
road still on him, to the captain's door.

It stayed closed a long time.

*Next: six more days before anyone is allowed to look at that draw.*

## Session 5 — The Vents

*387 AW. Three weeks of winter, a mountainside full of holes, and a stair for
somebody who hasn't arrived yet.*

The captain's door opened in the afternoon of the seventeenth day and Thale came
out of it having not changed his clothes in nineteen hours, and said the captain
wanted four of them in an hour, and somebody had better go and find Tarn.

Alinar spent that morning chasing the war. Ordow gave him the shape of it and
then he went and found a man it had actually happened to — a soldier called Oris
Venn, sat on the infirmary step with his arm strapped up because the ward smells
and the snow helps. No moon either night. Thirty Dark Elves who fought in the
dark like they live in it and then broke off all at once, as if somebody said so.
A blade that went through a shield and a chain sleeve and the shoulder behind
it, which he would not swear to in front of an officer and said so without being
asked.

And four giants who came up *behind* the line, stood in the field, and did not
chase anybody when Ferrow's company pulled back into the trees. They stayed with
the carts.

*Everyone's telling it as giants came down on us. They didn't come down on
anybody.*

Ignis spent the same morning finding out that you cannot walk the eastern
quarter of Caldalus with Sharii's wolf, because the wolf has drawn a circle with
Sharii at the middle of it and will not discuss the radius. What she could reach
was a glassblower on the Low Weaving called Phessa Molt, who finished looking at
her instead of looking away quickly, and sold her a restock of everything except
sulphur — because there isn't any. Three men from the Office of Works bought out
the shops a fortnight ago, and then the crown bought whatever came in behind
them, and Phessa didn't ask either of them why because she'd like to keep
sleeping.

---

The captain had cleared his desk — properly cleared it, everything put somewhere
else on purpose — and ruled a sheet of paper down the middle.

What a man saw on the left. What it means on the right. *And they do not go in
the same letter.*

He wanted the east written as *the giants stood in the field and did not
pursue*, because that cannot be argued with, and any man who knows his business
will arrive at *somebody is controlling them* on his own and think he thought of
it. *That's not me softening it. That's me loading it.*

Mya asked the only question nobody had. **What did they take?** Because a raid
takes what it'll carry and burns the rest, and it doesn't post four giants on a
cart while thirty soldiers go chasing men into trees. *That's not plunder.
That's a load. Somebody set a guard on it.* And if somebody's hauling, there's a
line, and it has two ends, and we've been staring at the other one for a
fortnight.

He wouldn't write that either. But he sent east for the grain schedules, as the
most boring administrative question a man can ask, because if what they took is
food then somebody is feeding something and he would like to know how many
mouths.

Maevis had three days of fury in a case on her knees. The scholar who couldn't
read her rubbing was honest and she said so. The man who took it wasn't college
— plain clothes, no seal, a crown writ she wasn't allowed to read properly. And
an old porter on the annex door, who likes company in the cold, says he'd seen
that same gentleman *two days before.* Before anybody had shown the college
anything at all.

She asked him twice and got a different number the second time, and reported
that herself before anyone could use it on her.

*I have spent three weeks worrying that nobody above me is listening*, the
captain said. *It had not occurred to me to worry that somebody is.*

He kept her notebook of remembered inscription overnight and copied it out by
hand — badly, on purpose — so there'd be two bad copies in two buildings instead
of one, and neither of them would be the single point at which it stopped
existing.

Then he signed the levy petition. Nineteen hours behind that door, and what came
out of it was: Thale was right about the clause, wrong about the order of it,
and punishing a man for finding the only lever in the county means forty-one
people fall off a roll in March to make a point.

And he told Mya to her face, in front of witnesses, that her name was on page
one of a letter to her father. Not asking. Telling, so she wouldn't find out
afterward.

*Name everyone,* she said. *Same weight, no rank on any of them. If I'm the only
name he recognises, it won't matter what you wrote.*

---

They had days to fill, so Ignis took the wolf out to a hedge line to teach him
to hunt, and found out he had never been taught — orphaned at eight weeks when
the fighting scattered the pack, and everything he knows he learned off an elf.
He has the nose. He has no patience and no idea how to run at where a thing is
going to be, and on every single approach, in the last twenty yards, **he looks
back at Sharii** — which costs him half a second, which is the half second the
hare uses.

Told to stop, he didn't refuse. He couldn't understand the question. *But then I
wouldn't know.*

She tried the other way round and pretended to attack his master, and found that
he was never fooled for one second, and that he doesn't threaten and doesn't
pursue — he just *stands in front*, every time, and will not leave the spot even
to meet something at twenty feet. One move. Already perfect. And on the seventh
pass, when she came in like she meant it, something answered in his chest before
he did and made a sound none of them had heard, and the whistle that has never
once failed **took three.**

He wouldn't eat from her hand that night. He slept on Sharii's boots.

So she spent ten minutes as a wolf, talking to a wolf, and told him it had been
pretending, and found that he'd known that all along and it was never the
problem. The problem was that *you came the way the thing comes*, and that
something had happened in him that he didn't choose, and that he knows it took
three.

She told him he was right to stand in front. That helped.

And then he asked her something, which he has never done.

Sometimes Sharii **goes somewhere without moving.** The wolf knows the instant
it happens — he knew it once across a drill yard with his back turned — and he
does not know it by smell, and his whole world is smell, and he has nothing to
compare it to.

*Is he coming back?*

The spell ran out in the middle of it.

---

Mya had asked for a calendar instead of more weather, so Ignis got one. Two
hours of Maevis complaining and refusing and then doing it anyway: the floor two
to four hundred feet down, good stone which means slow stone, one to three
treads a day a pair — and two holes she couldn't close, because nobody knows if
they sleep and nobody knows how much is already cut.

**Two months to a year. Most likely spring.** *That's not a figure. That's a
season, and a season is a worthless thing to plan around.*

But she was certain about the stonework. Shallow rise, deep tread — a cathedral
pitch — and wide. Four or five abreast, two hundred feet down, by hand, in the
dark.

*Nobody cuts a cathedral stair four abreast for one slow old man. That's a stair
for a column.*

And Mya, standing at the butt range in the last of the light, worked out the
other half: the draw shuts in January. The stair finishes in spring.

**Faelyn goes blind for exactly as long as it takes them to finish, and walks
back up that mountain the week after it's done.**

---

Then Dalla Sarn came down off the hill a day and a half early with ice in her
eyebrows and a report that said *nothing has happened* — twelve days, nobody in,
nobody out, no light, no sound. And a second page, in Wick's hand, which was a
map.

Twelve days of lying snow and a trapper with nothing to do. Snow falls on that
mountainside everywhere and there are places where it doesn't stay — dark wet
ground, green moss, in the third week of winter. He had walked the whole visible
face at four hundred yards and marked every one.

**Eleven.** Running east and a little north. The furthest the better part of two
miles out.

*You could not see these in autumn. There was nothing to see them against.*

*That hill is breathing out of eleven places. We found one of them and thought
we'd found the thing.*

---

The orders came down with something new in them: an end on every line. Daylight
only. Stop if the weather closes. *Eleven is a number, not a target* — nine is
an answer. Anything wider than a man's shoulders, ten paces and walk away. And
the whole order expires in fourteen days whatever's left undone.

*I have about twenty people who do exactly what they're told*, the captain said.
*And I have been writing orders for forty years without once putting an end on
one of them.*

He'd got that off a nine-year-old with a splint.

Six of them walked all eleven in four days. Every live one the same: bare warm
ground, clean edge, living moss — and **nothing has ever walked on any of them.**
In a mountain stripped of every animal that can leave, the most attractive patch
of ground for miles, untouched. And beside every single one, the loose stone
that should have choked it taken out and set down in a tidy heap a yard to the
left.

*Somebody comes out here and cleans it. Eleven times. Along two miles. And they
come up from underneath, because there's no track on this snow but ours.*

At the fourth one you could put your ear to the rock and hear **tools.** Very
faint, very regular, a long way down. Maevis lay in the wet moss for a long time
and came up with a number: three pairs working, not in step, which is how you
know it's three and not an echo. She counted again two days later at another
vent and got three again.

It was almost dark and they were still at it.

At the same vent the thing happened to Sharii, out of nowhere, and put him on
one knee — the same cold vast structured thing he felt at the bottom of the raw
tunnel three weeks ago. **And it was no weaker**, two-thirds of a mile away on a
different face of the mountain.

*Then either it's enormous*, Mya said, *or we're walking along the top of it.*

The seventh vent was dead. Choked, grey, a season gone — and an old cleared heap
beside it, overgrown, from the last time anybody did the job. *They maintained
this one, and then they stopped. A year ago. And they've kept the other ten
since.*

The ninth was two feet across, which is wider than a man's shoulders, and the
order was read out loud and obeyed. They marked it, took a bearing from
distance, and walked away from the most interesting hole any of them had ever
seen.

And somewhere in those three days Ignis stopped asking whether the wax tablet
was still in the junction and started treating the question as a *method* —
casting into every hole she had a spell left for. Ten of them gave nothing.

**The fourth one answered. Down, and north, and not moving.**

---

Dovin spent an evening in Aldric's kitchen with a rule and a candle and put all
eleven on paper, and they are not in a line. They sit on a long shallow curve,
and when he took three of them and constructed the centre, and then did it again
with three others, the two answers landed a few hundred yards apart.

North. Deep under the high Peaks, past the seam, past the draw, past anywhere
any Wood Elf has walked in living memory. And the radius so large that eleven
vents over two and a half miles are not a circle at all. They're a scrape along
the edge of one.

*The lamps were laid in arcs*, Sharii said.

And Maevis took the whole thing apart before anyone else could, because that is
what she is for — faults curve, cracks follow the rock, and a centre constructed
through three holes by a man with a rule in a farmhouse kitchen is not a place,
it's arithmetic.

*All of which is true. And I'd also tell him that a natural fault doesn't have
somebody walking two and a half miles of it with a shovel, stacking the spoil on
the left-hand side every single time.*

---

The captain heard all of it in one sitting without interrupting, and then stood
and looked at his wall for a while.

*Four months ago this company's whole business was a goat trail and two giants.*

And then he told them what he meant by asking more of them, which was not a
compliment: that he has no engineers and no scouts and one healer, that they are
the only people in Faelyn who have been down there, and that in five weeks
they've handed him four reports and every one contained the thing that weakened
it. *Men write what makes them look right. You three keep handing me the knife.*

So he will reach for them every time. And somebody will have to be the first
person back up that mountain after the thaw, into a hall with eleven vents and a
staircase four abreast that somebody spent a winter finishing, and he is not
going to pretend he doesn't know who.

Then he turned to the one of them who isn't Faelyn, and isn't sworn to anything,
and told her — once, in front of witnesses, while nothing was on fire — that she
can go. Any week of this winter. Because the next time he asks her for
something, he wants to be certain she's there by choice.

---

In the morning they asked to go after the tablet. Not to take it. To see where
it went.

Maevis found the flaw in a minute — the tablet answered at the *fourth* vent and
they were proposing to climb in at the *ninth*, a mile further on. The captain
found the second flaw and it was his own: nobody knows what that hole does past
the lip, because his own order had six obedient people stand ten paces back from
the only measurement that mattered.

Thale killed the fallback outright. *A fallback is an instruction with no
condition on it, agreed in a warm room and carried out by tired people at the
end of a long day. The stair is its own question. They don't get to fall into
it.*

And Mya asked what nobody had.

*We left them a thing, on purpose, after an argument. And they came and fetched
it and nothing bad happened. That's the only exchange anyone in this kingdom has
ever had with them. And now we're proposing to go into their house in the dark
and take it back. I'd like somebody to tell me what sentence that is.*

*I'd go. But in fifteen weeks they have never once raised a hand to us, and if
that ends, I want us to be able to say honestly who ended it. Because at the
minute, it would be us.*

So the order got written, with its own ending on it, the way they all do now.
Everyone goes. Everyone holds at ten paces. One scout, one hour, counted out
loud from outside and called at forty — and the one line the captain actually
cared about:

**Go no further in than you can still see daylight behind you. The moment you
can't see the opening, you turn round.**

Not *when you judge it's getting risky.* A thing your eyes tell you, not a thing
you have to decide.

*Next: six people north in the deep of winter, and a hole nobody has looked
into.*

## Session 6 — The Ladder

*387 AW. Twelve days. Caldalus to a two-foot hole in a mountain and back again,
and nobody drew a weapon once.*

They marched out of the gate yard at an hour when the captain had no business
being in one. He came anyway, looked at the Tiefling for about a second and a
half, and said nothing at all about the door he'd left open behind her, which
was the only honest thing available to him. Kharis Ailwin lifted a mug an inch
to Alinar from the armoury step. Then six of them went north under an order with
its own ending written on it.

On the third day out they walked into nine adult wolves on a fresh kill, eighty
yards off the road. Knight gave his silent warning and then went anyway, because
the whistle has never once been able to hold him — and what stopped him was
Ignis arriving beside him in the shape of a white wolf, which is a fact about
this squadron that Sharii said out loud afterwards as a limit of his own rather
than a credit to hers.

What they got out of that pack was more than Faelyn has gathered in four months.
No young — not one animal under three years old, in the third week of winter.
Two broken packs holding together under a torn-eared female who leads because
she was the one who got them down. And they weren't hungry, which meant there
was nothing to trade them; the only currency was distance.

*Nothing came for us. I would know a thing that came for us.*

*We did not leave. We stopped being able to stay.*

It's in the ground, she said. Not a smell, not a sound. You can't get away from
it sideways, only downward. It began before the leaves went. And then the thing
nobody was ready for: *it did not grow. It stepped.* More times than she can
count, and every step shed more animals, which is how two packs became one
group. Worst high, and north, and deep.

The tall silent ones that smell of under? She's walked past one close enough to
touch and it didn't turn round. They were there before it got bad, and they've
never hunted anything. ***They are not it.***

The giants have always been up there. There are fewer.

And then she asked them something nobody in this kingdom has asked: *The
feeling. Is it coming down?*

Neither of them had an answer.

In Aldric's kitchen that night it turned into an argument about speed, and the
argument got away from everybody. Ignis said she wanted to go back in and
*talk* to them — and then left the room so the others could say no without her
in it, which Mya named immediately as the opposite of what the last person to
call a meeting had done. Thale refused the giants outright with his name on it.
And when Sharii put a harder sentence in his brother's mouth than the one his
brother had said — *let's speed this up before more people die* — Thale didn't
ask who. He named them. Marrin Sowle. Hesk Varran. The two Arrowood brothers,
who went into the grain store after seed corn that burned anyway.

*We were late because nobody knew, not because anybody dithered. None of what
we've got now came from moving fast. Which doesn't make you wrong. It means
we're both right and only one of us gets to be right in time.*

And Mya: *We were late to Ashwell because we were looking for the wrong thing.
We're not late to this one. We're EARLY. That's the only advantage anybody in
this kingdom has got.*

And Aldric, from his own doorway, having lost nobody of his own: *It'd be US,
and it'd be me counting them again in the morning. It's my village. I'd like a
vote in the sentence.*

---

Then the shoulder, and vent nine, and the order read out loud, all nine lines.

She went in as a badger. Four feet of natural crack, fourteen inches at the
tightest — and then it stops being natural. Cut walls. Tooled, squared, lightly
domed. A floor sloping down and **worn smooth in a band eighteen inches wide**,
by feet, a great many, over a very long time. No inscription anywhere. The smell
of people soaked into the stone, continuous, and no food, no waste, no latrine,
and nothing has ever denned in the warmest driest hole on that mountainside.

Thirty feet in it bends right, and pale steady flameless light lay on the wall
of the bend. Going round it meant losing sight of the opening, which was the one
line the captain actually cared about.

She went round it. She'd decided to before she went in.

A domed room, hand-cut, fifteen feet across. A three-foot stone cube covered
edge to edge in that script so finely it reads as texture — more writing on that
one object than on the whole three hundred and fifty feet of corridor. A light
standing a hand's width clear of it, touching nothing, lighting nothing. A
circle cut into the floor around it, two-thirds finished, stopping mid-character,
the chips swept into a tidy heap a hand's span to the left. Tools laid out in a
row by size, in the order you'd pick them up.

And in the far floor, a square-cut shaft going down past sight, with hand- and
foot-holds chiselled into opposing walls and worn smooth.

**The vents are the tops of ladders. That is how they get to the surface.**

She put a paw on the cube. It is body-warm and it is not stone — it gives by a
hair, like a drumhead. Her body locked rigid for six seconds. The light
flickered once, and these lights do not flicker. And somebody started climbing,
unhurried, perfectly even, the pace of a person on a ladder they have used ten
thousand times.

She turned back into herself and waited, and chose a spell, and did not cast it.

Six foot four. Translucent white skin with the vessels visible in the throat.
White hair tied back. Entirely black eyes that take light and keep it. Four
breaths a minute, no weapon, no armour, and the hands of a lifetime stonecutter.
It stood still for three seconds on arriving, walked past her within four feet
without looking at her, and put its own hand flat on the exact face she had
touched — and nothing happened to it.

Then it turned and looked directly at her, and held it.

Then it held out one open hand, palm up, empty, at chest height. And waited.

She showed it the river stone, and **it took one breath out of rhythm** — half a
beat early and deeper, then straight back to four a minute. In twenty weeks of
prisoners and sealed doors and working ledges, that is the only involuntary thing
any of them has ever done.

It didn't want the stone. The hand stayed open. So she put her palm in it: cool
as a cellar wall, dry, split nails, heavy callus, old scars — and the pulse is
not in it. Then it turned her hand over and moved it toward the cube, slowly,
with every chance to refuse, and stopped four inches off and waited, looking at
her face rather than her hand.

She went the last four inches herself.

And because its hand never left hers, the thing went *through* her instead of
seizing her, and she felt the shape of it for the first time. The cube is not
the source — it's somewhere the thing passes through. It reaches north, and
down, and much further than she has any instrument for. There are others. Many.
And some are not doing it yet; there is an absence in it with a shape.

It is not sending anything. No horn, no drum, no signal, no warning.

**It feels like strain. Like a rope under load. Like standing with a hand flat on
a dam. Something very large is holding something, very carefully, and has been
for a long time — and what bleeds off the edge of it is what emptied the
mountain.** Nothing in it is in a hurry. Nothing in it is angry.

Then it lifted its hand off hers, looked at her, looked at the passage, and
looked back. *Go.* No threat in it — the gesture you make to somebody standing in
a doorway you need. Then it knelt, took the third tool from the row, and started
cutting four feet from her without looking up again.

And every tool in the shaft started again at once, settling straight back into
the three rhythms Maevis has been counting from a mountainside, as though
something had been waiting to be told it could.

She came out through fourteen inches of rock as herself, on her side, one
shoulder at a time, to save a Wild Shape the party might need. Out at
twenty-nine minutes on a forty-minute clock, with skin off her forearm and a
long tear in her coat.

Knight would not come near her, and stood behind Sharii's leg for the whole
report.

*It had you by the hand, thirty feet inside a mountain, and nobody would ever
have found you,* Mya said. *And it put you on the stone and then it pointed at
the door. I asked three weeks ago what sentence we were writing. I still don't
know. But that one isn't ours any more — **they answered.**

---

They went down off the shoulder the same afternoon and the weather shut the
mountain behind them that night.

In Ashwell, Thale heard the whole thing — breaches first, because she always
leads with those — and said he wasn't going to stand there and be right at
anybody, and then found the problem: his own letter asking the captain whether
they might make contact was going to land one day *before* the news that it had
already happened. So the party carried the news south themselves.

Behind the byre, in the sleet, she asked the wolf why he wouldn't come near her.

She smells of underneath. Not like it — *of* it. The cold-inside-of-a-stone
smell; the one his mother had on her the nights she moved the den downhill, one
at a time. And she still smells of herself at the same time, and he has no shelf
anywhere in his head for a thing that is two smells at once. He isn't frightened
*of* her. He's frightened *near* her, and he insisted on the difference twice.
And the only thing his mother ever taught him about that smell was to move away
from it, and it's the only instruction another wolf has ever given him, and he is
carrying it out on a friend.

*I do not want to. I am doing the only thing.*

So she gave him a job: tell me when it's gone. He was on his feet before she
finished and invented a procedure in about nine seconds — once a day, downwind,
same hour, same mark, starting the day after tomorrow, because he's honest about
what he can manage. To do the job he has to walk toward the thing he was taught
to walk away from. Every day. On purpose. He took it anyway, because jobs are the
only shelf in his head that holds.

Then Sharii spent a slot and for four minutes all three of them were in the same
conversation, which this party has never had. What got through was the part that
mattered: *she went to the underneath on purpose, and she came back.* Knight
stopped moving and repeated it twice, because it breaks the rule he inherited.

Four minutes earlier he had asked her whether she was coming back, and Sharii,
who doesn't know that question was asked, had just answered it.

What didn't get through was the six. He turned it over and couldn't find anywhere
to put it. He has Sharii, and the ones who are with him — a radius with a centre,
not a circle of equals. *I am with him* was singular on purpose. And then, with
no idea he was correcting anybody: *She was already with him. She has been since
the rain.*

The granary yard. Five weeks ago. Her belonging was never the question.

At the end of it he crossed four feet of frozen mud and smelled her for two
seconds with his weight back and his ears flat, and retreated, and sat down
facing her.

---

Two other things happened at Aldric's long table while the weather screamed off
the shoulder.

Alinar walked the engineer over to Dovin Reyl and sat them down about the seal
on the writ that took her rubbing — the lead that had been sitting in the open
for three sessions, which Maevis had never pulled because she had the surveyor
filed under *boundaries.* Two seconds, upside down, across a table, was all she
ever had; made to draw it, she got one usable number out of it. Two word-breaks
in the ring. Dovin handles five crown dies and every one of them carries a long
office name — four breaks, five, six. *Two is not an office.* Which means
household, or privy, or something cut for a person rather than a desk. Which
means it was never registered, and the register everybody had been planning to
ask has nothing to be asked.

So Alinar said: then don't ask who cut it, ask who's *allowed* to. There's a
schedule of warrants bound into the back of the plan-office standing orders.
Nobody guards a list of seals; it isn't a secret, it's a reference. Dovin will be
in that office inside the month anyway, because Ashwell's boundary needs a
clerk's hand, and he set his own limits before anyone asked him to: he'll look at
the page, once, in passing, and ask nobody anything, and shut the book if it
isn't there in the open. *The errand is real. That's the whole of what makes it
safe.*

And then Ignis sat down with Maevis and two candles and tried to put the floor of
that room on paper. The writing was never going to come back — she had seconds,
in bad light, with a stranger holding her hand — and Maevis said so for her
before she could apologise for it. *You were not there to copy a wall. You were
there to not die.*

What came back was the shape. A single band, cut into the floor. Characters
facing inward, toward the cube, all of them. The gap on the side nearest the
shaft, where somebody coming up the ladder would stand. One partial character at
the stop, three strokes and the beginning of a fourth — which Maevis refused to
compare against her own remembered notebook, because two bad copies agreeing is
not a match, it's two people remembering the same way.

Then she asked a flat question for her own reasons. *Was the lamp lit?*

It was.

And Maevis Tarn sat back in a farmhouse chair and said that in the autumn
everybody — her included, reading the report — had the order of it the same way
round. Box, circle, lamp, finished thing. And that lamp was burning over a circle
two-thirds cut.

**The writing isn't what makes it work.**

She took her own finding apart inside thirty seconds — two observations, maybe
lamps just burn where boxes are, maybe the circle is a dedication or a date or a
receipt, *I took Dovin's arc apart for less* — and then wrote it down anyway and
signed the page.

She got two more things out of it. That the cube was set down first and the
circle cut around it, which you can read off where a man has to kneel. And that
the stop and the tools disagree and she will not reconcile them: the cut stops
*mid-character*, which forty years says no tradesman does — and then he laid his
tools out by size in pick-up order anyway. A man who tidies his tools didn't
choose that stopping point. Something stopped the cut and then gave him all the
time in the world.

And last, the thing only the drawing surfaced. She'd thought the spoil swept a
yard to the left at every vent was a habit. A habit is one man. That's a
*standard* — identical above ground and below, over two and a half miles, by more
than one pair of hands.

*I sweep left because a man named Heddick Vull cuffed me for sweeping right when
I was forty years old. That's how a trade gets into your arms. Somebody puts it
there.*

*They don't speak. They don't look at each other. And somebody* taught *them
where to put the chips.*

Nobody at that table had a follow-up.

---

They made Caldalus a full day early, because somebody finally thought to ask the
druid what the weather was doing. It turns out Druidcraft will tell you the next
twenty-four hours, and nobody had used it once in four months. She called a front
under a hard clear sky on the second day and they pushed three hours before noon
and took the sheltered lee, and were tucked in with a fire going when it sat on
the road for six hours. Dry ground under every bedroll, a fire that caught first
time, and on the last night a single white anemone opened out of frozen turf
beside Maevis's boot. Maevis looked at it a long while and didn't say anything at
all.

Nine miles short of the gate they met the answer coming the other way: a courier
on foot, nineteen years old, eleven days in the service and the wrong boots,
carrying a sealed sheet for Sergeant Thale. The captain's ruling on whether they
might make contact — written by a man who didn't know the question was five days
dead. He wouldn't hand it over and Mya told him he was right not to, in front of
everybody. What he'd give them was the shape: *he asked me how fast a man can get
north in this weather. Twice.* And one piece of city news. There'd been a rider
from the palace at the company office two days back.

Nobody spoke for about thirty yards. The second letter went south with Mya's name
in it at her own insistence.

*Two bits of paper passing each other on a road in the dark,* Wick said, *and
both of them are wrong.*

---

She chose to report that night, at the eleventh hour, with the road still on her.
*Every hour I hold it is an hour I spent deciding how to tell it.*

He read it twice and asked four questions and not a fifth. Then he told them what
he'd written north, which was *yes* — yes to answering what they leave and
leaving things to be answered, at the junction or the seam, in daylight, nobody
out of sight of two other people. A day of his life to write it, five days too
late to matter.

*I gave permission for a conversation. And you had a meeting.*

Then he stood up and took his own winter apart.

*I have been writing stopping conditions into every line of every order since the
autumn and I have been insufferable about it, and I got the idea off a
nine-year-old with a splint. Coll Fenn did exactly what he was told, all the way
down. That is the boy I built the whole thing on.*

*A stopping condition is not a wall. It is a promise. It works on a person who
wants to stop. I have spent three months congratulating myself on fences and what
I had was a line of people being polite to me.*

*And the first time I put one in front of somebody who had already decided to pay
for crossing it, it did exactly nothing. It told her the price.*

*The only person in my company who has broken one of my conditions is the one
person I deliberately made it impossible to discipline. I disarmed myself in
October and I was pleased with myself for it. I'd do it again. That's the
trouble.*

No charge. No reprimand. The breaches go to the king in his own hand, in the
left-hand column, unsoftened — *my first letter was my judgement; this is evidence
against my judgement; I don't take the knife out.* And then he asked her the thing
he had no right to ask: not whether she was sorry, and not whether she'd do it
again, but **what condition she would actually have obeyed.**

She told him the truth, which was useless to him. The only order she'd have
followed was to trust her own instinct, which told her there was nothing in that
room that meant her harm — and that it wasn't selfish, because the people outside
were in it too.

He wrote it down word for word before he argued with it.

*An order has to be the same sentence for everybody who reads it. Trust your
instinct is not a sentence. It's a name. Put that in an order and in six months a
nineteen-year-old with the wrong boots reads it at the mouth of a hole and it
tells him that* his *feeling is an instruction. And he'll have one. And he'll be
wrong, and the order will have told him he wasn't.*

Then the room went to work on her, which is what that room does. Mya, who said
three weeks ago that being pulled somewhere and being right to go are two facts
with the same shape: *I was wrong for sixty years about what I was being pulled
toward, and I was never once unsure while I was wrong.* Maevis: *a thing that has
never been wrong is not the same as a thing that cannot be wrong, and your sample
is one room* — and then, immediately, *and I can't take apart the part that
matters, because she was in there and I wasn't.* Alinar counted it out flat, no
adjectives: a watered prisoner, a door shut from inside with the rubble carried
away, nine of them stopping and going back to work, one following five hundred
feet carrying nothing, a tablet fetched and nothing done about it, a hand held in
a room with nobody else in it. *That's not an instinct, sir. That's the
best-evidenced thing anyone in this kingdom believes about them.*

And Sharii said one sentence and never defended it: *My thing is right that
something's there and wrong about what it is nearly every time.*

What the captain found in the end was already his. He laid the autumn order beside
the winter one and saw that he'd been running two doctrines at once — forward
judgement for one scout, and an eyes-only stopping condition for another, handed
to her in the one place on that mountain where nothing can be seen from outside at
all.

And he found her condition in her account rather than her answer. At the end of
that room a thing looked at her, looked at the way out, and looked back, and she
went — no argument, no delay, with the only piece of information this kingdom has
in her hands. *That is the one instruction you have ever been given in that
mountain that you obeyed inside a second. It wasn't mine. I'd be a fool to write
anything else.*

*Captain,* Maevis said, *paragraph three puts our stopping condition in the hands
of the thing we're studying.*

*Yes.*

*You know how that reads.*

*I do. And it is the only line on that page that the record says will actually
work.*

---

The other thing waiting in Caldalus was the east.

The palace rider had come about page two. Somebody, somewhere in four hundred
miles of paper, had finally set *the Dark Elves broke contact all at once* beside
*four giants stood in the field and did not pursue* — and the answer was a
request for four experienced soldiers out of this company, before the end of the
month, specifically people who have stood in front of a Stone Giant and lived.

Which is this squadron. Not a conspiracy. A clerk doing his job with the only
information anybody sent him.

The captain did not pick four names quietly. He pinned the whole request up on
Ordow's board where the duty lists go, with one line under it in his own hand:
*If you want to go, come and say so to me. If you don't, say nothing and nothing
will be said.*

Asked straight out where the greater need was, he gave the answer and then told
them to discount it. The east, and it isn't close — men died there this month.
And the east has an army on it, and four bodies out of this company will matter
enormously to the eleven men standing next to them and won't move that war by a
hair. Nobody is dying on the mountain. By any measure he was taught it isn't a
need at all. *And there is nobody else.*

*East, if you want to be useful. North, if you want to be necessary. Those are
different things and only one of them feels like anything while you're doing it.*

*Now discount it. That mountain is mine. You have just asked a man whether the
thing he has staked his name on is important, and he has said yes.*

And then he said the part he wouldn't hide: if the people who've been down the
hole stay, the four names he writes are the four he can most afford to lose from
the work in front of him — the youngest, the newest, the ones with the least to
put in a report. He did not say the name. *It's not east or north. It's east, or
north and somebody else goes east instead. And I'm aware that telling you that is
a lever, and I'd rather you had it than not.*

So Alinar put the question to the squadron, in the mess, at midday, in front of
everybody. Is there anyone here who is absolutely certain they want to be one of
the four?

Kharis Ailwin answered before the question had finished. *Yes.* And then, because
Alinar waited, and because Alinar is the only person in that squadron who has ever
waited for Kharis Ailwin, he said the rest of it out loud for the first time in
front of anyone.

*The paper says they want people who've stood in front of a Stone Giant and are
still here. There's four of them in a field out east and nobody there knows what
to do about it. I came to Caldalus to tell somebody what happened to my village. I
stayed because of what did it. I don't care which end of the kingdom they're
standing in. I'd walk to Fardar.*

It is the most anybody has heard him say. Mya looked at the table and said
nothing whatsoever.

Tam Ellisan put his hand up. Actually put his hand up, and went red about it, and
said it anyway. *It says people who've stood in front of one. I was at Ashwell. I
know I didn't do anything much — I was on the left with a spear. But I was there.
I'm on the list. That's the only list I've ever been on.* And then: *the captain's
going to write four names and everyone in here knows which four he can spare. So
I'd rather say it than be told it.*

He is not wrong about any part of that.

Neva Corrin wouldn't answer, and the way she refused was the answer. *Don't make
me be certain about something in a room. Ask me that on a road and I'll tell
you.* Wick wanted the north and said out loud that his reason was selfish. Mya
restated hers once and refused to perform it twice — and found the thing nobody
else had: there is no date on that request. The one company in Faelyn that has
made a religion out of putting an ending on every line it writes has been handed a
piece of paper with none.

*Whoever goes east — nobody in this room can tell them when they're coming back.*

Sharii said no to the split. Not this one. Ever. Mya told him, levelly, that
they'd been split three times in four weeks and none of it had been their choice,
and that he wasn't arguing against a plan but against being in a militia. And then
Kharis, quietly, to nobody in particular: *He's not talking about the company.
There's two of them and they've never been more than a field apart in forty-five
years. That's not a tactical position. Don't argue him out of it like it is one.*

Sharii didn't look up. Nobody picked it up.

And into the silence that left, Ignis jerked her thumb at her own chest and said,
*Oh, now look who can't keep his big mouth shut* — and the whole table fell on it
gratefully, Neva first, and for about a minute this squadron was just a group of
people eating lunch. Fifth time somebody has named Sharii's habit to his face.
First time it was a kindness.

Afterwards she caught Neva in a corridor and said what she actually meant: that
Neva is ready for a fight, and north is standing outside a hole twiddling her
thumbs.

Neva went for the joke, didn't get the exit, and found the one true thing
available. *You've never seen us fight. Six weeks. A sealed door, a ledge, eleven
holes, a wolf on a road. Not one arrow loosed in anger since you walked into that
granary yard. You've never once seen this squadron in a fight, and you've just
told me which of us is ready for one.*

And then, for exactly one sentence, the mask came off.

*I went down at Ashwell in the first ten seconds. I don't remember any of it. I
know Mya stood over me because other people told me, and I know Sharii put my feet
back under me because he keeps the receipt. The best shot in this squadron has
never knowingly been in a fight. I was asleep for mine. Everyone tells me I was
brilliant right up until I wasn't.*

*So no. I'm not certain. I don't know what I am yet, and I'm the only one in that
mess who doesn't.*

Then it slammed shut, and she went down the corridor, and stopped, and said
without turning round: *And I'll go and tell him myself. Today. Because if that
line's going in his file I'd rather it had my name on it than yours. And don't you
dare look pleased.*

She went to the captain's door that afternoon and put her own name in.

---

They brought him an honest, incomplete report — two volunteers, two for the
north, nobody else committed, Sharii against any further split, and Ignis's own
view that Neva should go, which Neva had declined to say in a room. And the four
names were his.

He took it, and told them exactly what he'd do with it. Kharis and Tam in the
left-hand column, where what a person *said* goes. Ignis's view of Neva in the
right, where assessments go, with her name on it. And then he'd go and find Neva
and tell her to her face that it was there and who had put it there, *because I'll
not run a company where people discover their own names secondhand.* And one
observation, offered because somebody should: *this is the first time you have put
a name in instead of keeping one out. I'd think about the difference between those
two afternoons sometime.*

Mya said nothing, and did not look at Ignis.

On Tam: *that boy has done the arithmetic I did last night and got the same answer
without anybody helping him. His volunteering changes nothing about whether he
goes and everything about the paper. What it changes is that he gets to have
chosen it, and that is the only part of this a man actually keeps.* And then the
one thing actually in his power: *a detachment is not four separate people. It's a
shape. Whoever I put round him decides what his odds are.*

On the missing date he wrote to the palace that same hour, two sentences, and
credited Mya by name in the body of it. It has not been answered.

On Sharii he made the only promise he thought he could keep. *I can't promise you
we'll never be split — if I promised it, the first time the war needed it I'd break
it, and you'd have learned that my promises have a condition on them I didn't
write down. Here's what I can promise. I will not split the two of you by
accident. If it happens it will be because I decided it, for a reason, and I will
say it to the both of you myself, in a room, before it's on any list. That is a
smaller thing than you asked for. It's the size of thing I can actually hand you.*

And at the end: *I have spent this entire winter handing authority down to
recruits. And this afternoon the three of you walked in here and handed one back.
Which is correct — naming four people out of twenty is a captain's job. I'd just
like it on the record that I noticed, and that I don't yet know whether what I'm
looking at is good judgement or relief, and neither do you.*

Alinar asked him one more thing before he left, which was whether there'd still
be a chance to be useful in the east after the northern business was finished.

*Yes. Almost certainly yes, and I want you to hear how badly that should comfort
you. A war does not run out of places that need you. It runs out of the people who
were willing to go.*

And then: *after the business in the mountain is finished. There is no finish on
that. There's no line anywhere in that file that says what* done *looks like. So
waiting for it is an instruction with no condition on it — and my own sergeant
killed those, and I made it a standing ruling, and I signed it. If you're waiting
for the north to be finished before you go east, you are not deciding. You are
postponing, for ever, with my blessing.*

*You have wanted the east since the autumn and you have never once asked me for
it. I noticed. If you ask me, you go. But you have to ask. I am not going to do
you the favour of putting you on a list so you never had to.*

Alinar said thank you, sir, and left.

The board went up on the forty-ninth. Ailwin. Ellisan. Corrin. Kade — Ohren
volunteered within the hour and told the yard about it twice. Two shields, a bow
and a spear, *because a detachment is a shape and not only four heads.* They march
on the twenty-eighth. There is still no return date.

The twins are not on it, and the captain sent for them both before the sheet was
pinned and said so to their faces. *It would have been convenient to. One of you
east would have given them a second blade and a tracker and cost me almost nothing
I can measure. I didn't do it. Remember that I chose not to, because there will be
a day I choose the other way, and I'd like you to know the difference when it
comes.*

Mya is not on it. Kharis is. They have not spoken about it in front of anybody. On
the forty-eighth they were both at the butt range for about forty minutes, at
opposite ends, and neither of them shot particularly well.

And then four of them stood in an office while the captain read paragraph nine out
loud before anybody touched the pen.

*The judgement is yours, and the consequence is held where the judgement is. No
person acting under this order shall be described, in any report or inquiry, as
having been following instructions. There are none to follow.*

*An order is a thing that carries the blame. Coll Fenn rubbed his grandmother's leg
raw for a fortnight and not one person in Ashwell thinks it was his fault, because
he was doing what he was told, all the way down. That boy is protected by the
sentence somebody gave him. If the judgement is yours in writing, there's no
sentence between you and it. Not for me to hide behind, which is fine, I'm paid for
that. And not for you.*

And there was a half-second when every person in that room expected him to reach
for the easy thing — *you can walk out of that gate any week of this winter* — and
he didn't. He said it once in October, deliberately, and he was not going to use it
as a lever now that it would work.

She signed. Then each of them said *nobody follows you in* to her face, out loud,
because Alinar had insisted the clause be spoken and not merely initialled, and
Sharii said it last and took the longest about it. And then the captain signed
under all of them and dated it — the first time in forty years of writing orders
that he has been the last signature on one instead of the first.

Out in the yard, Bellis Ordow had the pencil out.

He'd left a space beside her name since the night she came in out of the rain,
because *support* is an honest word and also a word that lets a person not say a
thing, and because *that's hers to tell me.* He filled it in without asking her —
one word, which he'd got off the captain on the way past the door, because he
writes down what he's told and he hadn't got a word of his own for a person who
goes out of sight.

**IGNIS — judgement.**

She asked him, annoyed and not quite sure why, what one would even write in that
spot. And he told her she had the board the wrong way round. It isn't what people
are good at. It's where to look for you when it's gone wrong, and it has never
been anything else. *Kade — forward. Corrin — where she can see the whole of it.
Pallowen — not where you left him. Ailwin — between. That's the only one I've
never had to change.*

And four spaces up, blank since the autumn — *not because I'm being delicate. He
hasn't done anything yet. He's going east on the twenty-eighth and I expect I'll be
filling it in off somebody else's report, and I don't like that, and nobody asked
me.*

*It's a pencil,* he said. *Everything on this board is pencil, because people
change and I'm not in the business of being right about them for ever. I've rubbed
out more words than I've written. I rubbed out* bear bait *about four months after I
put it up, and the lad never knew it was there.*

*Two hundred years of this and I am very rarely surprised. I'd like it on the
record that I was.*

Then he held the pencil out to her, blunt end first. *If you've got a better word
than his, put it up yourself. It's your spot. I only keep the board.*

She rubbed his word out with the side of her thumb and wrote her own.

**IGNIS — where she was told not to go.**

He read it, and said *huh*, and put his tally board down, which he hadn't done all
morning. *I'm not rubbing that out, and it isn't because it's brave. There are
nineteen entries on this board and yours is the only one that's actually an
instruction. If you go missing, nobody wastes an hour looking where you were
posted. They go and read the order, and then they go to the line in it that says*
don't. *I have never had a better one of those off anybody.*

And then he added two words in his own hand, which is the one privilege the job
carries.

**IGNIS — where she was told not to go — and back.**

*Because that's the whole of my trade and it has been since before your
grandmother. Things that go out and come back are stores. Things that go out and
don't are losses. You went down and you came up. That's the bit you left off, and
you'd have left it off, because you lot hand me the knife every time and think it's
modesty.*

*It's pencil. If it ever stops being true I'll rub out the last two words myself
and I'll not enjoy it.*

Then, to the sacks: *Surprised twice now. That's a record.*

Across the yard the wolf was sitting at the foot of the armoury step with his nose
into the wind, at the hour, downwind, waiting — because somebody gave him a job and
he does exactly what he's told, every day, at the same hour, to the exact mark.

She walked over to him, and he didn't move away.

*Next: four people east on the twenty-eighth, a draw that shuts in January, a
staircase that finishes in spring, and nothing written anywhere that says what done
looks like.*

## Session 7 — The Correspondence

*387 AW. Days fifty to a hundred and three. A whole winter's worth of it, and
nobody drew a weapon once.*

They waited five days to be ordered and the order never came, because the captain
had decided not to send for them and was waiting to see whether anybody would walk
through the door. Bellis Ordow broke it in the end, over a tally board: *that
order's been sat in his folder five days with nothing in the top line, and he's
not going to fill it in.*

So they went and filled it in. Vaeroth threw out the first version — *that's three
objectives and a wish* — and Maevis gave them the three questions that could
actually be answered by somebody with eyes and a date. Alinar picked the broadest
of them, fast, to get the conversation over with, and the captain noticed out loud
and let it go.

Before they marched, four people went east. Ignis handed out ten berries that would
be food for exactly one day, and told them so, which is a strange thing to give
somebody leaving for an unknown number of them. Kade ate his on the spot. Tam put
both in his coat and ate neither. Kharis said thank you — the only one who did —
and then, quietly, *Tam. Eat them today.* He lifted his chin an inch to Alinar from
the armoury step and went. Mya stood in that yard the whole four minutes, nine feet
away at the closest, and neither of them said a word.

---

Meanwhile a wolf was being taken apart and put back together.

In wolf shape Ignis gets everything — nose to nose, asleep against her, the stick
brought all the way instead of dropped halfway. The second she turns back into
herself he is at two feet again. Asked why, he explained it better than anybody
else in this company could have: as a wolf she is *one* thing, a wolf that smells
of a place, and he has a shelf for that. As herself she is two things at once.
*It is not more frightening. It is less of a shape.*

Told she was going back under, he did not argue. He asked to come, was told no,
and then folded it into the job he already had. *I will be at the mark at the hour
every day the whole time you are under.*

And then he asked her the thing again, with a new fact attached to it, because he
had been turning it over for five weeks: *you go to the underneath and you come
back. He goes somewhere without moving. Is that the same place? Does he come back?*

**She told Sharii.** All of it, including that she had held it since the autumn and
had no good account of why. And Sharii, who takes everything flat and asks the next
useful question, asked whether he smelled of it too.

*No. He has no under on him. Not ever. Not once.*

And then the part nobody was ready for. It isn't a smell at all. **When it happens,
Knight cannot smell him.** Everything else in the world is still there — the snow,
the smoke, the horses, the man with the sacks. Sharii is not. *There is a place
where he is standing and there is nothing in it.* Eleven times, he says, and he
cannot count past three and is certain it is eleven.

*I do not think he is going under. I do not know where he goes. There is nothing
there to know. That is the whole of why I asked.*

Then, to Sharii, who had never once been told: *It does not hurt me. I would like
to be told before it happens so I can be where you are. I know you cannot tell me.
I would like it anyway.*

Nobody said anything. There were four minutes of spell left and nobody used them.

---

Vent four nearly didn't let her in at all. Ten inches of live rock at the pinch —
a badger gets through that and nothing else does, and a badger leaves fur on the
walls doing it. Beyond it a twenty-inch crawl, polished in a band down the middle
and rubbed smooth on both walls at the height of a passing body, which means
something comes through there tight and often and has for a very long time. And at
the end of it, an opening near the *ceiling* of a room, eight feet above the floor.

Vent four is not a door. It's a hole in a roof.

The room had a cube, a lamp, and a circle that was finished. The pulse through it
was weaker than the ninth — the same rope further out along its length — and the
absence, the part of it that isn't working yet, was right there at the edge of it.

And in a cut recess at the shaft head, four feet up the wall: **the wax tablet.**
Theirs. Carried two and a half miles through a mountain from the junction where six
people left it in the autumn, stylus back in its spine, cleaned.

Nine lines. Edge to edge, margins respected, a hand sized for somebody to read it.
And in the corner, beside the mason's test-stroke Mya cut, **the same stroke
again** — same length, same angle, a finger's width apart.

*This is a surface and this is how you mark it.*
*Yes. It is. And this is how I mark it.*

---

Down the shaft, one of two rhythms stopped and the other didn't, and one of them
walked two hundred feet to fetch her. She showed it the river stone and got
nothing at all — not a half-beat, not a deeper breath. Then it turned side-on and
held its arm out along the passage, and waited.

Every order this company has ever written covers being told to leave. There is
nothing anywhere about being asked to come.

What it took her to was a chamber with no light in it, a cube that was warm and
soft and **completely silent**, a finished circle around it — and a second, larger
circle being cut outside the first, half done, by one of them kneeling in the
pitch dark.

They finished a circle and it didn't light. So they're cutting another one.

She stood inside the absence and found that it is not a hole in something. It runs
on, north and down, more than one, and **the work is on its edge, cutting outward
into the dark part.**

She offered her hand and said *if you can understand me, take my hand.* It did not
take her hand. It took her by the wrist, turned her palm up, and drew one short
stroke across it with a finger. Then it looked at her, looked at the passage, and
looked back, and she left inside a second.

She took the tablet, and said so first, and came out through ten inches of rock as
a spider four minutes after Maevis had called forty.

---

Maevis sat up a whole night copying it by candlelight and called the copy bad and
made it anyway, because she has lost one of these before. Thale ruled that the
original and the copy do not travel on the same road. And then he asked the
question he had actually come into the room for: *in the autumn Aldric asked
whether there was anything coming down off that hill he'd want his people somewhere
else for, and we told him no. Is that still true?*

They told him. Not the lamps and the shelves — just that the answer to his own
question had changed from *no* to *we don't know*, which Alinar found the words
for.

He asked three questions and they were all farming questions. *When. Can we plant.*
And then, out of the mouth of a man who has never met a wolf in his life: ***is it
coming down.***

Then he gave three rulings, standing up. He tells the village, all of it, because
sitting on a thing is how a headman turns into a liar. Nobody goes up the draw,
including in the spring. And if it ever came to moving — forty-one people, no
chartered ground, one neighbour in the world who'd take them — *there's exactly one
place, and the only reason he'd listen is a piece of paper your lad spent a
fortnight on about grain. I'd like it written so it'd work for something else.*

Everybody at that table understood the same thing at the same moment. The levy, the
boundary and the Dorn's Ford charter stopped being about food in that kitchen.
They are Ashwell's only evacuation plan, and they lapse in March.

---

In Caldalus the captain asked four questions and the last one was *if I write an
order that says no, will it work.* The answer was no, and everybody in the room
knew it before she said it.

*Twice now something down there has told her to leave and she has gone inside a
second. And nothing I have ever written has stopped her once. What I'm looking at
is a soldier who obeys instructions issued by something I cannot identify, have
never seen and cannot speak to. And it has been giving her good ones. That is the
most frightening sentence I have ever said in this room.*

No charge. No reprimand, and no explanation of why, because he spent that one last
time. He put the north above his own head in writing, with the tablet named in it,
and told her to her face: *there is nothing left between you and that hole that I
know how to write.*

On Ashwell he moved inside five minutes. The charter goes to the assessment **as
grain**, with the reason in no letter at all, *because the moment I write* they may
have to move *it becomes a thing a clerk can refuse.*

On the tablet he laid out three roads and let them pick, and when they picked
*keep it*, he refused to let them carry it. *I will not conceal it. I will delay
it, with a date on it, in writing, in my own hand. And I will not write down where
it is.* His date is March.

And when Alinar asked for standing orders or unwritten guidance for a winter of
watching, he refused both and named the thing the party didn't have a word for.
*An order that never ends is a fallback. An order that recurs, with a date on each
turn, is a rota.*

Ten-day turns. Eleven holes. Entirely surface. Ten paces and look. Weather beats
the order. A report south on a fixed day whether there's anything in it or not,
*because silence would otherwise have two meanings.* Expires when the draw shuts,
and everybody comes home.

And paragraph eight, which he would not soften: **nothing in this order authorises
anything inside the ground. There is no order for that and there is not going to
be one.**

Ordow worked out within a minute that a party feeding itself off a spell would
stop drawing rations and he would lose the only early-warning signal he has had for
two hundred years, and fixed it himself: *you draw anyway, all six, every turn,
whether you eat it or not. It's not rations. It's a heartbeat.*

They gave the stores to Ashwell. Aldric took them as **rent**, which is the only
frame that would have worked, and put them in the common store with Hesk Varran's
widow first. The twins fed the village without breaking sweat, because the hill is
empty and every animal that could leave it is down here in the hedges. *The only
reason Ashwell eats this winter,* Maevis said, *is the reason Ashwell might have to
leave it.*

---

Then a winter of walking the line.

And somewhere on the eighty-seventh day, standing over a hole with a thread in her
hand, Maevis Tarn got the first instrument this kingdom has ever had for that
mountain. **Every hole with tools under it blows warm air out. Every silent hole
draws cold air in.** Twelve for twelve across two turns, including two reversals
— vent six went quiet and reversed, and vent two, silent for months, woke up at the
far west end with one pair under it.

Which means it isn't a front marching east. **They open new ground.**

And one hole breaks the rule. **Vent eleven** — the furthest out, cleaned inside
the week every single time, the strongest and warmest draught on the whole line,
and *nothing under it at any depth.* Heat with no work in it. She wrote no
conclusion for it. Three times.

---

The exchange was Ignis's idea and it is the best thing anybody has designed all
winter: leave another blank board with the same stroke, and find out whether the
nine lines are a letter or a form. *We can't read it now. That isn't the same as
never.*

They told the captain they were going to do it, in advance, in writing, knowing he
had said he would never authorise it — which Thale named for exactly what it was.
*That is either the most honest thing anybody in this company has ever done, or it
is a way of making him responsible for it.* And then he put both intentions in the
first two lines of his own cover sheet and added *I did not forbid it. I could have
and I did not, and I want that in my hand and not inferred from my silence.*

They went without waiting for the answer. The answer is on the road. Second time.

---

Down the corridor alone — past the depot, past fifty feet of wall that was raw rock
in the autumn and now carries the same sentence running through it without a join
— she came out onto the ledge and found it empty. The nine went down with the work.

The staircase is real and it is magnificent and **nothing has ever walked on it.**
Every single thing these people use shows it — the crawls, the holds, the floors.
Not this. *It is for somebody who has not arrived.*

She walked it. Two hundred feet, in the dark, with the void open on one side and no
rail. And somewhere past the third turn she came level with the lamps, and they are
not where anybody thought. They are not on a floor. **They are on a wall**, on cut
shelves, at one height — and the stair goes past them and keeps going down.

*Nobody has ever seen the bottom of that place.*

Below the lamp line it gets warmer, and the pulse comes up through your boots
without you touching anything. The finished stair stops and becomes rough, and the
rough becomes raw, and on the raw there were nine of them cutting, in total
darkness, four feet away —

— and they did not react to her at all. In the autumn an engineer slipped eighty
feet from them and all nine stopped dead for three seconds. She walked down their
staircase out of the dark and stood at arm's length and nothing happened.

She held the board up. One stopped mid-stroke and the other eight didn't. It looked
at her, looked at the board, put one fingertip on the stroke in the corner, didn't
take it — and then looked up the stair, and back, and up again.

*Not here. Up there. We made you somewhere.*

At the head of the stair, cut since the autumn, at the height of a person's hands,
swept clean: a recess. Empty for three months. She put the board in it and **it fit
exactly.** Not nearly. They measured something and made a place for it.

*That's not a people being approached,* Maevis said. *That's a people waiting.*

---

Vent nine wouldn't let her through either, the first time. She got properly stuck,
had to empty her lungs and work backwards, and did it again the other way round —
fifteen minutes and both forearms raw, and from outside it sounded like scraping
and then forty seconds of nothing.

The room had changed. The circle that stopped mid-character is **finished** —
whatever stopped that cut let him come back and complete it. The tools are gone,
the chips are gone, the floor is swept. And beside the shaft head, cut since the
autumn, at hand height, sized exactly: another recess.

That's three. The ledge, the shaft head under four, and this one.

**They have built a post system.**

She left the board in it and went down sixty feet of ladder into a gallery longer
than she could see in either direction, with **twenty-four of them in it at least**
— she stopped counting where her sight ended and there were more past it — cutting
**shelves**, level, square, at a fixed interval, in total darkness, in silence, none
of them looking at another.

So the dark arc isn't a gap in anything. It's where they haven't got to yet.
*Detect Magic* in that gallery returned nothing whatsoever — not the workers, not
their tools, not the finished shelves. **They build the shelf before the thing
exists.**

She tried to lead one up. The nearest stopped, looked at the shaft, looked back at
her, and didn't move — her own sentence handed back. And then one she had never
approached, facing the wall, well out of sight behind her, set its tool down in the
row by size and climbed the ladder past her without a glance.

Nobody spoke. Nobody looked. The right one went.

---

She followed it up and found it **sitting on the floor of that room with the board
on its knees, writing.**

Four minutes. Left to right, margins respected, stylus held like a chisel, eyes on
the middle distance. And then the thing that answered the whole experiment without
anybody needing to read a word:

**It wrote the first several lines without one pause. Then it stopped for half a
minute. Then it wrote slower, and went back and corrected itself twice.**

A form does not get corrected.

Eleven lines. The first one was nine.

It seated the stylus, stood, put the board back in the recess — it would not hand
it to her — and looked at her, and looked at the niche, and went back down the
ladder.

And with a spell running the entire time, none of it was magical. Not the stylus,
not the wax, not the act. The cube three feet away never flickered once.

---

They compared the two on a folded cloak on a freezing shelf, and for the first time
Maevis was holding **two originals** instead of two bad copies, which is the only
reason she would say what she said.

**The first six lines are identical.** Then they diverge. One runs three more and
ends short; the other runs five more and ends short. Both corrections are in the
body. Neither is in the form.

*Six lines of form. A body. And a close. That is the shape of a letter, and I
cannot read one mark of it, and I have just described its structure off two
samples. That is actually how it is done.*

*They said more because something was different,* Mya said. *And the different thing
was her.*

---

Two days west, on the road, Sharii asked Mya whether she thought they knew we can't
understand them. She walked two hundred yards before answering.

*Every single thing that has ever actually worked between us and them has been done
with hands.* The stone on the floor. The water moved to where a blind woman's hand
came down. The open palm. The hand turned over and stopped four inches short. The
look at the way out. The fingertip on the stroke. *Not one of those was writing.
Every one of them worked. And they've written us twenty lines and not a word of it
has ever landed.*

*A people who didn't know we were deaf would keep shouting. These ones put the
shout on a board, and then take you by the hand for the part that matters. They
know.*

And then the worse half: *so why are they writing it? Somebody is meant to read
that. And I don't think it's us.*

*Or it's not for anybody,* Wick said. *It's a record.*

Maevis couldn't take either one apart, and said so, and wrote both down.

---

They came back to the ledge on the hundred and third day and board number four was
in the niche and it was not blank.

**Nine lines. Character for character, line break for line break, the same as the
original.**

Which means the place doesn't matter. Which means the nine lines are not a letter.
*You put a blank board with a stroke on it in front of these people and you get
these nine lines. It's a form. It's a receipt.*

The thing this company concealed from a crown. The thing a captain has a note in
his own hand about, dated March. The thing a sergeant split from its copy so it
couldn't be taken in one go.

A form reply.

And then Wick said *check the corner*, and nobody had.

The original has the answering stroke. The eleven-line one — handed over in person,
answered in front of a witness — has it. **The one left in a box, where nobody met
anybody, does not.**

*So that stroke isn't part of the letter. It's what passes between two people who
have actually met. It was on her palm. A finger, on skin, once.*

And then, because she is who she is: *except the original has it and nobody met
anybody. Six of you put that board on a floor and were a mile off before anything
came for it. I cannot reconcile that and I am not going to. Both halves go on the
paper.*

Mya turned the eleven-line board over in her hands.

*Then this is the only thing they have ever actually said to us. Three lines
different. Two of them fixed twice. And half a minute of a man on a stone floor
deciding what to put.*

*Next: a blank board, a ledge, six people, and nobody has decided what to write.*

---

## Session 8 — The Break

*387 AW. Two days. A board in a box, a stair with no bottom, and a road nobody
knew was there.*

They spent an hour on that ledge arguing about a single scratch in wax, and the
argument was better than the answer.

Sharii laid it out cleanly: either the mark was copied because a mark is a mark,
or because it was Mya's, or because they knew a mason's test-stroke when they saw
one — and only the first of those assumes nothing. Maevis took the baseline off
him gratefully and then turned the third one inside out. A test-stroke isn't
knowledge about Wood Elves. It's knowledge about *chisels*. It would be the same
act in their hands if Faelyn had never existed. Which makes it the smallest
assumption on the list and not the largest — and which means the first and third
readings produce exactly the same wax, for ever, and no board anybody can make
will ever tell them apart.

*There's only one of your three I can actually test, and it's the one with a
person in it.*

And Mya, who had said nothing the whole time: *Then take my hand off the next
one.*

Ignis wanted to put the stone and four words in eight languages on a board.
Alinar wanted it fast. Sharii changed his mind in the middle of the argument and
said so, and came out the other side wanting nothing on the wax at all — because
if there's no answer you've learned nothing and given everything, and you'll never
know whether the silence was *can't* or *won't*.

*Everything in life is a gamble. You have to pay to play, wolf boy.*

What went in the box in the end was one blank board with one stroke in the lower
right, cut by Ignis's hand instead of Mya's. Maevis put her own thirty-day
programme in the fire to do it — the control already existed in the satchel,
twice over — and said out loud, before it was cut, exactly what the board would
and would not prove.

Then she realised she had stood on that ledge four times this winter and never
once surveyed it, and was furious with herself for an hour and eleven minutes.

Fifty-eight lamps. Evenly spaced. All at one height, to the limit of what she
could read — *somebody ran a level out there; that's my trade, and they have
somebody who does it.* Not three arcs on a floor but one continuous line on a
curving wall with holes punched in it. And when they took her two hundred feet
down the stair and she ran the thing properly, the line curved away on both
hands and closed, and her centre landed in the same few hundred yards as the one
Dovin Reyl built with a rule and a candle in Aldric's kitchen.

*Two bad methods agreeing is two people being wrong the same way. Three bad
methods agreeing is three. And I have never in forty years had three bad methods
agree.*

They came out in the dark, and a wolf was sitting at the mark downwind at the
hour because it was the hour, and the reading was two feet, exactly as it had
been — after the deepest and longest she has ever gone. It doesn't get worse the
further down she goes. Sharii's eyes came up at that, and Ignis saw them come up,
and neither of them said a word.

---

In the morning they went back in, all six, which Alinar made everybody say out
loud was the third breach and the biggest one and nobody's fault but theirs.

*The last four times, one of us went and five of us stood somewhere safe and
counted. Today that stops.*

The stair goes down past the lamps — and the lamps are the cubes, on shelves in
the wall, close enough to touch from the steps, which nobody had ever realised
because nobody but Ignis had ever been level with one. Past them it gets warmer.
Then the finished work runs out, and the rough runs out, and there is a last
half-shaped tread and then there is nothing at all.

**The staircase has no bottom. It is four hundred feet long and it ends in the
air.** Wick dropped a stone and it hit something at four seconds and hit again
and went away and never landed. And the warm air that comes out of eleven holes
on a mountainside two and a half miles away comes up past that last tread, out of
the dark underneath, which means Maevis has been standing on the far end of her
own instrument all winter without knowing it.

They stood there nineteen minutes while she worked.

And then all nine of them stopped. Set their tools down in the row by size, each
at its own place, and stood. Not the three seconds of the autumn. They did not
start again. Four hundred feet of climb in complete silence, which nobody has
ever heard in that mountain — and then, eleven minutes after the party was off
the stair, everything in the whole place started again at once.

---

Then somebody looked down and noticed the ledge was worn.

Eighteen inches of polished stone down the middle of it, the way the crawls are
polished, the way the corridor is polished — by feet, a very great many, over a
time nobody can measure. The stair has never been walked on in its life. *The
ledge is the road.* And it has mouths opening off it at intervals, cut and square
and worn, running back into the rock: the galleries. Trunk, branches, ladders.
Which is why there has never been a single track on that snow, and why in six
months they have never once used the door this militia opened.

A mile along it, in the dark, with the void on one hand, eleven of them came the
other way.

Nobody in Faelyn has ever seen them travelling. Baskets, rope, two water jars,
and one carrying nothing. They passed at two feet in full lantern light and not
one turned a head.

The eleventh stopped.

It crossed to the wall side and stood in front of Ignis, and when she held out
the river stone it took one breath out of rhythm — the second time in the history
of this correspondence that any of them has done an involuntary thing, and both
times it was that stone. It didn't want it. It touched the stone with one finger.
Then it moved the finger two inches and touched the exact place on her palm where
one of them drew a stroke on her thirty-five days ago, where there is nothing and
has been nothing since before the solstice.

Then it held its own hand open and waited.

*Stone. Mark. Hand.* The longest thing any of them has ever said to anybody, and
not one bit of it on wax.

She put the stone away and gave it her hand, which is the first time in thirty-one
weeks that a person has started something and one of them has allowed it. And it
led her north, with five people and three lanterns following and Maevis counting
paces because counting paces was all she could do.

---

Four hundred paces, and the lamps stopped.

They had walked to the edge of one of the gaps — and to a place with no vent over
it, a mile north off the end of the eleven, where nobody standing on that
mountainside has ever been above. *This is off the map. Not the part we haven't
filled in. There is no map.* And still, under their boots, a worn road running on
into the dark.

Half a mile of empty shelves, finished and swept and waiting.

And then shelves that weren't empty. Cubes on them, correct in every particular,
**dark** — each one ringed by a cut circle, and a second circle outside that, and
a third half-finished with the chips swept left.

*They're installed.*

*They're installed and they are not lit, and somebody has cut three rings round
that one trying to make it work.*

Forty to sixty missing, Maevis had said the night before. Two-thirds done. She
took it back standing up. **They are not missing. They failed.** And nobody
anywhere can say whether this thing has ever been nearer finished than it is now.

---

A quarter-mile on, the wall ends.

A hundred feet of it, not cut — broken. Raw shattered rock where the dressed face
should be, shelves stopping and starting either side, the ledge itself gone across
the span. And heaped along the inner edge, swept tidily to the left the way they
sweep everything, a cartload of smashed cube with three or four characters still
legible on every shard.

Maevis read the fracture for four minutes and then gave a conclusion, which she
almost never does, because stone is the one thing she will sign her name to.

*That face was struck. The spall runs the wrong way for anything coming out of
the mountain behind it. The force came from the open side. From the middle.*

And Mya said the thing nobody had been able to say since the autumn.

*Then we're not outside it. That wall is between that thing and Faelyn, and we
walked in through the hole in the side of the house, and we've been strolling
about on the inside of it since September. We're in with it.*

Ignis took the guide's hand a second time, of her own accord, and it turned her
palm down and moved it to the broken face and stopped four inches short and let
her go the last of the way herself, exactly as one of them did with a cube under
vent nine in the autumn. There is no load in the broken stone at all. But she
found the two cut ends of the line, one on either side of the gap.

They are carrying more.

Maevis sat down on the floor of that ledge, which nobody has seen her do.

*That's an arch. You take a span out of an arch and the two beside it carry what
it was carrying, and they hold for a while because it's good work, and then one of
them goes. It doesn't fail steadily. It fails in steps, and the steps come
faster.*

*It did not grow,* Ignis said. *It stepped.*

A torn-eared wolf on a road told them that in the autumn, and said it began before
the leaves went, and asked them a question nobody could answer. Aldric asked the
same one in his own kitchen, in his own words, with no idea a wolf had got there
first.

*Is it coming down?*

*Yes,* said Maevis. *And faster each time.*

---

Then the guide crouched at the heap and picked one shard out of it — flat, the
size of a hand, three characters and the start of a fourth cut so fine they read
as texture — and put it in Ignis's hand and closed her fingers over it.

The first thing anybody in Faelyn has ever been given that was cut in stone. And
it came out of a pile of something that failed.

She went across the gap as a spider, because nothing else in that party could.
Halfway over, a slab the size of a door let go under her and fell, and five people
heard it start and nobody heard it stop. On the far side the ledge is not worn —
nobody walks that side — and the lamps pick straight back up and run on, burning,
as though nothing had happened. The first cube past the break is cracked corner to
corner and still lit, braced with five rings and more past the edge of sight.
Somebody keeps coming back to that one.

And the guide watched her the whole way across, and did not stop watching.

She went on north alone. Thirteen lamps, a third of a mile, no worn band. And
then no lamps ahead at all, and a second gap she could not see the end of, and
more of them working it than anyone has ever seen in one place — ranks of them in
the pitch dark, passing stone hand to hand, not cutting but **clearing**.

There was dust in the air. The heap was started and not finished.

That one is fresh.

And past it, the first three shelves have cubes on them, and all three are dark.

*Next: a Tiefling four inches off the floor with thirty feet of sight, fourteen
minutes into an hour, a third of a mile past a gap her friends cannot cross.*
